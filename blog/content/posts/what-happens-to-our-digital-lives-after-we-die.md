## Writing notes

- **Alternative title:** Leaving Instructions, Not Just Passwords
- **Subtitle:** Digital continuity involves privacy, consent, and people, not simply recovering files.
- **Angle:** Consider what others should be able to preserve, close, or continue when the person managing a digital life is gone.
- **Indirect connection:** Care work, relational privacy, and the difference between technical access and legitimate authority.
- **Reader:** Someone with everyday accounts and shared responsibilities, not only a large archive or a technically elaborate setup.
- **Core question:** How can we reduce the burden on others without assuming they should inherit unrestricted access to everything?
- **Working position:** Leave understandable intentions and appropriate routes to act, while respecting other people's privacy and verifying formal requirements.
- **Boundaries:** Not legal advice, a password-sharing recipe, a bereavement anecdote, or merely a discussion of photo backups.

## Context and distinctions

- Separate death from temporary incapacity or absence. Similar continuity problems arise, but permissions and formal processes may differ; verify each independently.
- Distinguish technical access, provider permission, and legal authority. Possessing a device or credential does not by itself settle what someone may do.
- Separate shared household resources, financial obligations, public work, and private correspondence. Different material can warrant different instructions and recipients.
- Treat messages and collaborative records as relational: they can contain other people's confidences, not just information belonging solely to the account holder.

## Possible opening

- Suggest a hypothetical list: a recurring bill, a shared document, a private conversation, and a website that needs renewal. Ask which actions each requires.
- Keep the tone practical and gentle; do not invent a death, family dispute, or experience of grief to give the argument emotional weight.

## Section-by-section outline

### 1. Start with the people left to decide

- Identify possible needs: stopping charges, finding essential information, maintaining a shared service, or deciding what should remain public.
- Ask how uncertainty compounds administrative work. Avoid assuming every family is close, safe, or agreed about a person's wishes.

### 2. Sort the digital life by purpose

- Propose a small inventory organised by action: preserve, transfer where permitted, close, or leave private. Allow undecided items.
- Separate records needed for practical administration from sentimental material; an archive need not be opened in full to resolve one bill.

### 3. Put privacy and consent alongside access

- Explore a private message involving someone still living; ask whether an instruction about the account also addresses that person's expectations.
- Discuss selective preservation and explicit limits. Avoid treating either total disclosure or total deletion as the morally obvious default.

### 4. Map authority and provider processes

- Investigate designated-contact features and deceased-user procedures only through current documentation; distinguish data retrieval from signing in as the person.
- Note where professional advice may be needed for formal authority or conflicting instructions. Do not promise that a personal checklist overrides law or service terms.

### 5. Plan continuity without creating new exposure

- Sketch an inventory containing service names, purposes, responsible contacts, and locations of approved instructions, rather than secrets copied into a public document.
- Ask how an authorised person would locate and use the plan safely. Flag security tradeoffs and avoid prescribing one universal credential-sharing method.

### 6. Make the plan understandable and revisitable

- Suggest reviewing instructions after major account or relationship changes; consider whether the intended helper is willing and has enough context.
- Include the option to decline stewardship, discontinue a project, or preserve less. Continuity should not silently become an indefinite duty.

## Counterarguments worth taking seriously

- **This feels premature or distressing:** Respect that response; offer a small starting point focused on one shared responsibility rather than a complete mortality checklist.
- **A password manager solves access:** Recognise potential usefulness; separate secure access mechanisms from authority, privacy choices, and knowing what action is wanted.
- **Loved ones should decide later:** Allow discretion; ask whether a few stated preferences would reduce uncertainty without attempting to control every future decision.

## Examples and research to gather

- Read current official deceased-user policies for two relevant services; verify required documents, available actions, regional differences, and policy dates.
- Inspect documentation for one designated-contact or emergency-access feature; verify what it grants, what it excludes, and how it is activated.
- Seek jurisdiction-specific official estate guidance and, where needed, qualified legal advice; mark inheritance and authority questions for verification.
- Gather guidance from a reputable bereavement or digital-legacy organisation; distinguish practical suggestions from legal requirements and commercial services.

## Personal prompts

- Which responsibilities would genuinely become someone else's problem if I could no longer manage them?
- What would I want preserved, and what would I prefer to remain private, including material involving other people?
- Who could I ask about helping, without assuming their consent or assigning them permanent maintenance work?

## Possible ending

- **Takeaway:** A considerate digital legacy makes intentions and responsibilities clearer, rather than simply making every account accessible.
- **Possible final question:** Would the people left behind know what I hoped they would do, and what I hoped they would leave alone?
