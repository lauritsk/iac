## Writing notes

- **Alternative title:** What Does Buy Mean on a Digital Bookshelf?
- **Subtitle:** Reading access, file possession, and lasting rights are not necessarily the same thing.
- **Angle:** Examine the gap between the familiar language of buying books and the specific permissions attached to a digital edition.
- **Indirect connection:** Consumer expectations, cultural memory, and the power to define an ordinary transaction's terms.
- **Reader:** Someone who likes digital reading but has never examined what a purchase lets them retain or transfer.
- **Core question:** Which practical freedoms should a reader reasonably expect when a store says buy?
- **Working position:** Digital convenience is valuable, but restrictions should be understandable before payment and preservation should be taken seriously.
- **Boundaries:** Not legal advice, a guide to bypassing DRM, or a claim that every digital book has identical terms.

## Context and distinctions

- Separate copyright in the work from possession of a copy and permission to use it. Neither paper nor digital purchase means owning the author's copyright.
- Distinguish retailer licences, subscriptions, library loans, and directly purchased files; do not collapse temporary access and purchase into one argument.
- Separate DRM restrictions from file formats and account requirements. An interoperable format alone does not establish transfer rights or unrestricted access.
- Keep legal permission distinct from technical possibility. Being able to copy or open a file does not settle whether a particular use is permitted.

## Possible opening

- Suggest a hypothetical comparison: a paper book beside a digital purchase screen, followed by questions about lending, moving, and keeping each edition.
- Prompt: ask what the word buy led the reader to expect, without inventing a purchase, loss, or dispute from Karl's life.

## Section-by-section outline

### 1. Unpack the promise on the button

- Begin with the ordinary expectations attached to a purchase: repeated use, continued possession, and some freedom to choose where to read.
- Contrast those expectations with the actual offer; reserve judgement until the selected store's wording and terms have been checked.

### 2. Separate the layers of a digital book

- Map the work, edition, file, format, reading software, account, and licence. Use this to prevent ownership becoming an all-or-nothing slogan.
- Show how a restriction at one layer might matter even when another layer is open; verify any concrete example before naming it.

### 3. Follow ordinary reader activities

- Ask about reading offline, changing devices, lending, gifting, annotations, and accessibility tools. Compare permissions and usability separately.
- Choose two activities for closer treatment rather than implying every reader needs every freedom or every seller prohibits them.

### 4. Consider the long-lived bookshelf

- Explore what happens when devices change, software support ends, or account access is lost; frame these as scenarios to investigate, not established incidents.
- Separate keeping readable text from retaining notes and reading history. Ask what a reader can export and what remains service-dependent.

### 5. Take authors and publishers seriously

- Explain the concern about effortless redistribution without treating all paying readers as likely infringers.
- Examine possible tensions among author income, pricing, distribution, accessibility, and preservation. Seek evidence before claiming a restriction achieves its intended goal.

### 6. Propose a clearer purchase conversation

- Outline a pre-purchase disclosure: what is supplied, where it works, what can be downloaded, and which permissions or dependencies remain.
- Consider DRM-free editions and transparent licensing as options, while making clear that neither automatically settles every legal or preservation question.

## Counterarguments worth taking seriously

- **Digital delivery offers real convenience:** Recognise portability and rapid access; ask whether those benefits require every restriction under discussion.
- **Restrictions protect creative livelihoods:** Treat the concern fairly; distinguish a plausible objective from evidence that a particular mechanism is effective.
- **Readers accept the terms:** Acknowledge informed choice; examine whether the checkout language makes significant limits discoverable and understandable.

## Examples and research to gather

- Capture a chosen retailer's current purchase wording and applicable ebook terms; verify region, edition, and date before describing contractual rights.
- Compare one publisher's documented DRM-free offering with a restricted edition; verify formats, download access, and actual licence terms.
- Seek authoritative jurisdiction-specific guidance on digital content and transfer rights; mark legal interpretation for verification rather than announcing a universal rule.
- Consult an authoritative format specification and a library or preservation organisation's guidance; distinguish technical longevity from legal permission to preserve.

## Personal prompts

- What do I honestly expect to be able to do after buying a book, and which expectations come from paper?
- Have I checked the terms of a digital edition I actually own, rather than assuming what they say?
- Which would influence my choice most: price, convenience, accessibility, lending, or long-term readability?

## Possible ending

- **Takeaway:** The useful question is not only whether a purchase counts as ownership, but which lasting freedoms the transaction provides.
- **Possible final question:** If buy means something narrower here, should the difference be visible before we pay?
